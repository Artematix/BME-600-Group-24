# Phase 2 research log: methods and why

This file is the manager's running record of method decisions. Each entry: what we decided, why, what we rejected, and the risk. It is the draft for the "justification of methods" part of the paper. Newest entry last.

## 2026-10-26. Full analysis: plan and reasoning

### Where we stand
- Accuracy by range, Parkes error grid, and a first constant-low-bias simulation exist (RESULTS.md, SIMULATION_RESULTS.md).
- Phase 1 (MAUDE) is done at the structured-code level. Top problem in serious reports = wrong readings (33 %).
- Midterm plan submitted. Final project due Dec 4, 2026.

### Goal of this round
- A complete, trusted preprocessing pipeline.
- Methods that match what a 2026 CGM accuracy paper is expected to use. Not more.
- A wider search for findings (demographics, wear day, time of day, rate of change), done with rules that stop us fooling ourselves.
- One main finding that answers the research question and links Phase 1 to Phase 2.

### Step 1. Pipeline first (decided)
- Decision: one shared module (`preprocess.py`) builds cached tables once. All later scripts import it.
- Why: every number in the paper depends on the same cleaning steps. One module = one set of rules, one data-flow table (rows in, rows out), one place to fix bugs. Reviewers ask for exactly this table.
- Rules it must settle: study window (day 0-329), duplicate rows, clipped values (39 / 401 mg/dL), calibration dependence, sensor sessions and wear day, rate of change, night flag, Shanghai header cleanup.
- Calibration dependence, in plain words: the Dexcom G4 is corrected using fingersticks. A fingerstick used to correct the sensor is not a fair test of the sensor. So we keep an "independent meter pairs" set that drops those. If the headline MARD barely changes, we say so and move on.
- Sensor sessions: the data has no "new sensor" marker (to be confirmed by the audit). We infer sessions from gaps and the 7-day sensor life, and we check the rule by looking for a peak at 7 days. If the peak is not there, wear-day results get a warning label.

### Step 2. Methods check against standards (in progress, agent review)
- Decision: keep MARD as the headline but add the metrics reviewers expect: percent within 15/15 and 20/20 by range (the FDA iCGM thresholds give a ready benchmark), Parkes grid (done), per-participant confidence intervals by bootstrap, Bland-Altman that respects repeated measures per person, a lag check (shift the CGM in time, find the shift that minimises error), and rate-of-change bins.
- Why: these are the standard toolkit in CGM accuracy papers. Using them makes our numbers comparable to published ones.
- Rejected for now: full sensor error models (Kalman / Facchinetti-style), machine-learning error prediction. Reason: more complexity than the question needs, and hard to explain to the team.
- Waiting on: METHODS_REVIEW.md for the exact list and priorities.

### Step 3. Fishing for findings, with rules (decided)
- The danger: many comparisons give false "findings" by chance. We fish, but with rules.
- Rule 1. Unit of analysis = participant. We compute per-person MARD or bias first, then compare groups. Never compare pooled readings as if they were independent (one person gives thousands of readings).
- Rule 2. Two lists. Pre-specified (from the literature): low range worse; wear day 1 worse; night worse; fast rate of change worse; higher HbA1c or variability worse; age; sex; BMI. Exploratory: education, income, insurance, site, prior CGM use, pump model, comorbidities, diabetes duration.
- Rule 3. Multiple-comparison control inside the exploratory list (Benjamini-Hochberg). Pre-specified tests reported one by one with confidence intervals.
- Rule 4. Minimum group size: 10 participants per group, else the result is "not testable".
- Rule 5. Report effect sizes with confidence intervals, not just p-values. Report null results.
- Rule 6. Every finding must be checked in both pair types (meter pairs and calibration pairs) and, where possible, in Shanghai.
- Why these rules: they are the cheapest way to keep an exploratory analysis honest, and they are easy to explain in one paragraph of the paper.

### Step 4. Simulation version 2 (planned)
- Phase 1 says the top real-world problems are wrong readings, signal loss, and early sensor death. So version 2 adds: low bias (done), high bias, dropouts, early session end, and lag. Drift over wear day only if Step 3 shows wear-day error.
- Decision rules: keep the simple thresholds (70 / 200) as the headline, add real bolus-wizard events as a second rule set. Why: thresholds are easy to explain; wizard events show what patients actually did.
- Monte Carlo over fault size, per participant. Report events per person per day with the middle half (IQR).

### Step 5. What counts as the main finding
- It must answer the research question (how much error flips decisions, and how often real sensors reach it).
- It must hold in both pair types, with a confidence interval, and survive the rules in Step 3.
- It must be one sentence a teammate can repeat.
- Best candidate shape: "Real reports show errors of size X (from Phase 1 paired numbers). In trial data, errors of that size flip Y decisions per person per day, and they are most common in the low range where decisions matter most."
- Second candidate: a subgroup result, only if it survives the rules.

### Risks written down now
- Data dredging (handled by Step 3 rules).
- Calibration dependence (handled by the independent pair set).
- Narrow cohorts: adults, mostly White US (REPLACE-BG), Chinese in-patients (Shanghai). We say this in every subgroup result.
- Shanghai has 37 low-range pairs. It is a comparator, not evidence on its own.

## 2026-10-30. Core accuracy v2 (ACCURACY_V2.md): a second contamination, and the real headline
- New data problem found: the "meter" table mixes real meter-log fingersticks with glucose values typed into the pump. In the CGM-only arm those pump entries are CGM values (they match the sensor almost exactly). So even the "independent" pairs were too kind to the sensor. Fix: the reference must come from the meter log (`source == "meter"`), and not be a calibration. The preprocessing module needs this built in; until then every script filters it. Lesson for the paper: the funnel from 475,502 pairs at 10.4 % MARD to the clean set is a story in itself.
- Funnel of MARD: 10.4 % (midterm, all pairs) -> 10.2 % (study window) -> 11.9 % (drop pump entries) -> 13.3 % (drop calibration fingersticks) -> 13.8 % (CGM+BGM arm, randomised phase; 33,467 pairs, 77 people, CI 12.3 to 15.7). Set B (all 226 people, 168,990 clean pairs) gives the same numbers with tighter CIs, so Set B is the better headline and Set A the plan-promised check. Decision: report both, lead with B, explain in one sentence.
- The arms now agree (13.8 vs 13.1 %), and calibration pairs are close (14.1 %). The earlier "calibration pairs are much worse" story was mostly the contamination. Removed from the narrative.
- Benchmark: against the FDA iCGM limits (by CGM value, lower 95 % bound) the G4 fails all seven criteria, two of them narrowly. Stated as a benchmark only: the G4 predates the rule and our reference is a home meter, not a lab.
- Candidate main finding (strong): 28 % of meter-confirmed lows (below 70) were not shown as low by the sensor (CI 24 to 33). Sensitivity for lows is 72 % at 70 mg/dL and 52 % at 54. False lows are rare (3.4 %). This is the decision-relevant failure, it is measured on real pairs, and it is exactly the harm path of the recall (a sensor that hides lows, or reads low when the patient is not).
- Low-range bias on the fair axis (average of both readings) is small but real: -4.9 % below 54, -2.7 % at 54-69, CIs exclude zero. Meter error cannot explain it: adding simulated meter noise pushes it toward zero. By CGM value the sensor reads 21 % low below 54, which is what the patient sees but is partly selection.
- Bland-Altman limits are wide (-67 to +57 mg/dL) and almost all spread is within person. Clustering changes the CIs, not the limits.
- Shanghai: 98 patients have pairs (14 have none). MARD 13.9 %, bias -7.4 % at every estimable level. T1DM 18.4 % vs T2DM 12.9 %.
- Surprise worth a sentence: fingersticks copied into the pump show lower error (10.7 %) than other fingersticks (13.8 %). guess: routine pre-meal checks happen at calm glucose; symptom-driven checks happen when glucose moves. The reference you choose changes the answer.

## 2026-10-30. Error drivers (ERROR_DRIVERS.md): the primary uncertainty is answered
- Answer: the low-glucose bias persists after lag correction and after removing calibration and pump-typed pairs. It gets larger, not smaller: 6.5 % low below 54 (CI 5.1 to 7.9) and 4.1 % low at 54-69 (CI 3.2 to 4.9), binned by average, meter-log pairs only. Uncorrected it was 5.1 % and 2.8 %. Reason: fast-falling pairs, where a lagging sensor reads high, had masked part of the low bias.
- Lag: the sensor trails blood by about 5-7 minutes (best shift 5 min on raw readings, 7 min on the interpolated trace; per-person median 7, IQR 5-9). Correcting it lowers MARD from 13.2 to 12.2 %. The rate-of-change effect disappears after correction (fast-falling bias +12.7 % becomes +0.7 %). So "rate of change" is lag, not a separate sensor fault.
- Wear day: MARD 15.2 % on day 1, 13.2 % on day 2, 11.8 % on day 7. 86 % of people are worse on day 1. The low-range bias is concentrated on day 1: -12.3 % below 54 on day 1 vs -2.3 % on days 2-6 and -0.6 % on day 7. This is the second candidate main finding. It links to Phase 1 directly: Abbott's report template states the wear day of the failure, so we can compare the wear-day pattern of real complaints with this trial pattern.
- Night: pooled MARD is higher at night (14.3 vs 13.2 %) but per person the median difference is zero. Night has twice as many large errors (over 40 %), in both directions. We report it that way, not as "night is worse".
- One mixed model (143,051 pairs, random intercept per person): baseline 12.7 points of error; below 54 adds 5.8; 54-69 adds 3.0; fast falling or rising adds about 6 (before lag correction); wear day 1 adds 2.3; night adds 0.9; study arm adds nothing.
- Regression to the mean, in one table for the paper: below 54 the bias is +29.8 % binned by fingerstick, -22.5 % binned by CGM, -5.2 % binned by average. Same pairs, three answers. The average is the fair axis.
- Shanghai: lag cannot be resolved with 15-minute readings. Reads 6-8 % low at every trend.
- Pump-typed CGM values confirmed independently by this agent: with them included, the "best lag" drops to 2 minutes, which is how the contamination showed up a second way. The preprocessing module must get a built-in `source == "meter"` rule.
- Decision: the headline pair set for the whole paper is "meter-log fingersticks, not used for calibration, in the study window": 168,990 pairs, 226 people, MARD 13.3 % (CI 12.7 to 13.9). The CGM+BGM-arm randomised subset (13.8 %) is the plan-promised check.

## 2026-10-30. Subgroups (SUBGROUPS.md): who gets worse accuracy? Nobody in particular
- Per-person MARD on verified fingersticks: median 12.3 % (IQR 10.7 to 14.3), 226 people. Low-range MARD: 17.6 % (150 people with 20 or more low pairs). The low range costs about 5 points for everyone.
- Pre-specified covariates (age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, duration, arm): none associated with MARD after correction for multiple tests. The six-term model explains 2 % of the spread. Adding glucose variability and time below 70 changes nothing, except the weak age link flips sign because younger people have more variable glucose.
- What does predict error: the person's glucose pattern. Time below 70 and glucose variability (CV) each correlate 0.33 with MARD, and the same holds in calibration pairs. Message for the paper: sensor error follows how often and how fast glucose moves, not who the patient is.
- The low-range penalty (+5 points) is the same in every age, sex, BMI, HbA1c and arm group (all differences within about 1 point). Nobody is spared; nobody is singled out.
- Nulls that matter: sex, arm, HbA1c, and 26 weeks of use make no difference. The false arm difference seen before (+1.9 points) was entirely the pump-typed CGM values.
- Exploratory list: 22 tests, one passed correction (more listed conditions, lower MARD) and vanished after adjusting for age and variability. Treated as chance and said so. Race, income, education described, not tested (14 non-White people).
- Shanghai: 76 patients with 10 or more pairs. No group differences; CIs 4 to 9 points wide.
- Honesty rule applied: the strongest "finding" in this analysis is the data-quality one (per-person MARD 9.9 to 12.3 % once pump-typed values are removed). The paper states it.
- Follow-up for the pipeline: add an `is_verified_fingerstick` flag to preprocess.py (meter-log source, not a calibration) and make it the default pair set, so no script has to repeat the filter.

## 2026-10-30. Simulation version 2 (SIMULATION_V2.md): decisions, not readings
- Unit of analysis is now a decision: 159,878 bolus-calculator corrections (203 people) and 47,250 true low episodes (below 70, at least 15 minutes) over 50,775 participant-days. The dose rule reproduces the pump's own recorded correction within 0.15 units in 90 % of records, so the model is faithful.
- Headline lookup (median across people): a sensor reading 10 % low skips 0.2 % of corrections and changes almost no dose by a unit. At 20 % low it skips 7.2 % (IQR 4.3 to 11.0), changes 16.7 % of doses by more than 1 unit, hides 51 % of high episodes above 180, and adds 0.86 false low alerts per person per day. At 30 % low: 20 % skipped, 71 % of highs hidden, 1.3 false lows per day. Skipping starts near 12 % low bias.
- Why 10 % does so little: 10 % of 200 mg/dL is 20 mg/dL, which is 0.4 units at a typical sensitivity of 50. Doses move in bigger steps than the sensor error.
- Fault families at matched size: a constant low bias hurts most. Drift to the same bias by day 7 does about half the damage. Noise barely moves doses but is the only fault that hides low episodes, by breaking them into pieces shorter than 15 minutes. Dropouts hide lows in proportion to time lost. Lag only delays alerts.
- 180 is the headline high threshold (the research question's number, same as time above range); 200 is reported as a check and tracks it within a few points. Real correction triggers are the pumps' own "correct above" levels (median 120), so 180/200 crossings are alerts, not doses.
- A low-reading sensor flatters control: time in range rises from 63 to 75 % at 20 % low bias while the low-glucose risk index LBGI rises from 0.95 to 2.33. This is a point for the discussion: the fault hides itself in the usual summary metrics.
- Who is hurt most: false low alerts fall most on people with lower average glucose (rho -0.74 with mean glucose). The best-controlled patients get the most false lows. Skipped corrections correlate with no patient trait.
- Reality check with real pairs (CGM+BGM arm, verified fingersticks at dose time, 15,034 decisions): the unmodified G4 already changes 4.2 % of corrections by more than a unit. Adding 20 % low bias raises skipped corrections to 12 %.
- Version 1 vs 2: version-1 counts were 15 to 30 times larger because one hidden 2-hour high was counted 24 times. The paper shows the comparison table once and uses version 2.
- Join to Phase 1: simulation_v2_lookup.csv has every outcome at each bias from -40 to +40 % in 2 % steps. When the report-text extraction gives the real distribution of (sensor - meter) errors, we weight the lookup rows by it to get "expected decision impact of the errors actually reported".

## 2026-10-30. Main finding chosen (FINDINGS.md)
- Main finding: in real home use the sensor was least reliable exactly where a reading changes what the patient does. It missed 28 % of true lows below 70 mg/dL (CI 24 to 33), and 38 % of the lows it showed were not confirmed by the meter.
- Why this over the others: it is measured on real pairs, not simulated; it holds in all three pair sets (28, 31, 33 % missed); it has a confidence interval; it is one sentence; and it is the recall's harm path (a sensor that hides lows or shows lows that are not there).
- Honesty note carried into the paper: the G4's errors at the low line run both ways, while the Libre 3 recall fault was one way (reads low). We connect them through the simulation and the report texts, not by claiming the G4 is the Libre 3.
- Supporting findings, ranked: (1) dose decisions change at about 20 % low bias, not 10 %; (2) the low bias survives lag correction and grows; (3) lag of 5-7 minutes explains the whole rate-of-change effect, and wear day 1 is worst and holds the low bias; (4) sensor error follows glucose pattern, not demographics; (5) a low-reading sensor flatters time in range while the low-risk index rises.
- Plan promises: 10 done, 5 done differently (each explained), 1 not done (judging the simulation against iCGM limits makes no sense: they are agreement percentages, not decision counts), 1 in progress (report texts).
- Plan claims that changed: the midterm low-range MARD of 18.7 / 41.8 % was inflated by binning by meter value on contaminated pairs (now 16.4 / 19.1 % by average); "simulated low bias mostly causes missed highs" is now balanced (1.19 missed highs vs 0.86 false lows per day at 20 % low); the arms agree, so the CGM+BGM arm is a check, not the only valid set; and we now have 159,878 real dosing records, so "we cannot speak to actual treatment decisions" is partly withdrawn.
- Paper figures: 15 (misclassification, main finding), 13 (accuracy by range), 18 (low bias with lag correction), 20 (wear day), 25 (decision impact vs bias), 26 (fault families). Figure 28 (time in range vs risk index) is next in line.

## 2026-11-06. Deep demographics check (DEMOGRAPHICS_DEEP.md): the null holds under every angle we could think of
- Why: the first pass (SUBGROUPS.md) said who the person is does not predict sensor error, only their glucose pattern does. That surprised the project lead, so this run tried everything the data hold: 67 participant-level variables (demographics, money, insurance, body size, diabetes history, hypoglycaemia-awareness and hypo-fear questionnaires, conditions, medications, insulin, pumps, prior CGM, site, run-in), 7 error measures (MARD, low-range MARD, bias, low-range bias, missed-low rate, false-low rate, within 20/20), 18 pairs of traits, clinic site, flexible models, mediation chains, the worst quartile, and Shanghai.
- Method rule: the whole test list was written into the stats file before any test ran, and every result is in the memo, nulls included. One Benjamini-Hochberg family over all 680 REPLACE-BG tests; Shanghai is a second family of 60.
- Result: 69 raw p < 0.05 where 34 are expected by chance; one passes correction. In the low range the sensor reads about 2.5 mg/dL lower in women than in men (bias -6.4 vs -2.2 %, difference +4.2 points, CI +2.0 to +6.0, BH p 0.016), and women have fewer missed lows (27.5 vs 33.9 %, not significant after correction). It repeats in calibration pairs and after adjustment, but it is small, has no mechanism, and is weak in the randomised CGM+BGM arm (+1.8, CI -1.6 to +3.6). Filed as a lead, not a finding. Low-range MARD is the same in both sexes.
- Everything else is null: all 18 interactions (every 2x2 cell between 11.3 and 13.0 % MARD), site (ICC 0.035, permutation p 0.10; shrinks to 0.013 once the patients' glucose pattern is in the model), and the flexible models (random forest, gradient boosting, ridge: out-of-fold R-squared below zero for demographics in every run; glucose pattern alone 0.03 to 0.14; adding demographics on top makes every model worse). The mediation chains exist but are tiny: younger age -> more glucose swings -> higher MARD carries 0.3 MARD points per 14 years of age.
- Two follow-ups done ad hoc and labelled as such: the sex result (section 12a of the memo) and "more medications, lower MARD" (rho -0.24, BH 0.05), which vanishes after adjusting for age and glucose pattern and is absent in calibration pairs, exactly like "more conditions, lower MARD" in the first pass.
- Mistake caught on the way: a robust-error Wald test of site x arm on the missed-low rate gave p < 0.001 because several site-arm cells hold 1-3 people (robust errors explode there). Replaced by a plain F-test with a within-site permutation p (0.21). Lesson for the paper: never trust a robust-error test on cells that small.
- Shanghai: no site or hospital column exists, so location cannot be tested there; 60 tests on 20 traits, nothing (smallest BH p 0.97). Cohort tables for both datasets are now in one place (memo section 2).
- What changed in thinking: nothing in the conclusion, a lot in confidence. "Sensor error follows the glucose pattern, not the person" now rests on 680 tests, two cohorts, three model types and a site model, not on six covariates. The paper can state it plainly and move on. The one caveat to carry: the cohort is narrow (94 % White, 77 % with a degree, HbA1c near 7 %), so groups outside it were never tested.

## 2026-11-06. Sex follow-up (DEMOGRAPHICS_FOLLOWUP.md): the low-range sex gap is a sensor effect, small, and unexplained
- Why: the deep check left one lead: below 70 mg/dL the G4 reads lower in women than in men (bias -6.4 vs -2.2 %, men minus women +4.2 points). The project lead asked whether it is real, what explains it, and whether it matters.
- Method rule: 74 tests listed before any ran (66 REPLACE-BG, 3 Shanghai, 5 MAUDE; three BH families), every result reported. Splits with too few people for the per-person test fall back to pooled pairs with a participant bootstrap and a mixed-model p, a rule fixed in advance. One post-hoc block (blinded run-in, testing habits) is labelled as such and kept out of the families.
- Where it is: in every low-range split that can be measured (+3.3 to +4.8 points): both glucose bands, wear days 2-7, day and night, flat and falling glucose, lag-corrected pairs, calibration pairs, and "low" defined by the fingerstick (+5.5) or by the sensor (+2.4). Zero in 70-180, reversed above 180 (-0.9), gone by 100 mg/dL. Meter type is not testable (99.9 % Contour Next).
- Sensor, not person: women and men go low equally often, equally deep by fingerstick, at the same hours and speeds (16 measures, none pass). Matching the 10,022 low pairs on glucose bin and trend leaves +3.7 (CI +1.9 to +5.6); a mixed model with night, wear day 1 and arm gives +3.4. The only sex differences in the lows are the CGM-measured ones (deeper CGM nadirs, more 39-floor readings in women), which a lower-reading sensor produces.
- Not body or physiology: adjusting for weight, BMI, height, HbA1c, age and insulin dose per kg (TDD from the pump log, 0.51 vs 0.56 U/kg) leaves +4.0 (CI +0.9 to +7.0); within each sex none of these correlate with the bias. Haematocrit does not exist in either dataset (REPLACE-BG's central lab has HbA1c only).
- The caveat that stays: absent in the randomised CGM+BGM arm (+0.6, CI -2.5 to +3.5; 24 women, 12 men), sex x arm p 0.02 (BH 0.05). Post hoc: present in the blinded run-in where nobody could see the sensor (pooled +5.8, CI +2.0 to +9.9; calibration pairs +9.1 per person), so selective fingersticking does not make it. In the CGM+BGM arm the men rarely tested while low (2.9 % of their tests vs 6.3 %).
- Outside: Shanghai is uninformative (50 low pairs). MAUDE Dexcom complaints with a sex (7,030 pairs) lean the same way by 3-4 points (reads low 49 vs 45 %, hidden low 36 vs 38 %). Literature: no published sex difference in CGM low-range bias; the one study that checked (Libre, children, Arch Dis Child 2017) found none.
- Does it matter: about 2.5 mg/dL at 60. Lookup: no dose changes; +0.17 false low alerts per day in women. Real pairs near 70: women's sensors show 5 points more false lows (36 vs 31 %) and miss 5 points fewer true lows (37 vs 42 %). An alert-line effect, not a dosing one.
- Decision: one sentence in the paper, worded as a small unexplained sensor difference; not a finding to build on. What would settle it: a lab-reference (YSI) dataset with a newer sensor, both sexes in the low range, haematocrit and sensor site recorded.
- Lesson: the per-person rule (20 pairs, 10 people) runs out of people fast in thin splits (night, falling, rising); fixing the pooled fallback before running kept those splits honest instead of silently dropped.

## 2026-11-06. Wear day 1: the sensor settling in, not bad sensors being discarded (WEAR_DAY.md)
- Why: ERROR_DRIVERS.md found the G4 worst on wear day 1 (MARD 15.2 % vs 11.8 % on day 7). The project lead asked which story explains it: (a) bad sensors pulled early, so day 1 is full of bad sensors, or (b) every sensor settles in. The MAUDE texts (Abbott complaints name day 1 twice as often as a flat rate) made (a) plausible.
- Method, sessions: the cache infers sessions from gaps of 115 min or more and has no sensor ID. For this question that is not enough, because a signal gap inside one wear makes the first piece look like a sensor that ended early. Rule added in `wear_day.py`: a piece with no start-up calibration is glued back onto the session it follows (same sensor after a gap) unless the whole session would pass 170 h (the G4 stops at 7 days). 2,102 pieces glued; the recomputed wear day equals the cache value on 100 % of confirmed pieces. The raw-session version is kept as a sensitivity (same answer: selection 12 % instead of 8 %).
- Method, statistics: one shared participant bootstrap (1,000 draws of the 226 people, seed 20261106) for every number, so every difference (day 1 minus day 2, short minus long) has its own CI. Selection and settling are split by one subtraction: day-1 MARD of all sessions minus day-1 MARD of the sessions that reached day 7 = selection; the rest of the day-1-over-day-7 excess = settling. Settling time from a floor-plus-exponential fit on 6-h bins and from a plain "stays within 1 point of the final level" rule.
- Result: 92 % settling (3.0 of 3.3 points, CI 85 to 100), 8 % selection (0.25 points, CI 0.5 to 15). Sensors pulled on day 1 do read 4 points worse, but they are 7 % of sessions and 3.5 % of day-1 pairs; sensors that lasted 2 to 6 days were not worse on day 1 at all. The same sensor drops 2.0 points from day 1 to day 2 (median per sensor +1.35, mean +2.05) and 3.0 points to day 7; 81 % of people show it on their own sensors. Time constant 34 h (CI 25 to 48): two thirds of the extra error is gone after day 1, within half a point by about 72 h, flat from day 5.
- Direction: day 1 is noisier, not biased (bias -0.9 % vs -0.5 %; errors over 20 % are 1.5 times as common, both directions). Exception: in the low range a new sensor reads 10 % low on day 1, fading from -14 % in the first 6 h to -7 % at 18 to 24 h and gone by day 2. That doubles false lows on day 1 (4.5 % vs 2.3 %) and does not change missed lows (28 % vs 31 %).
- Calibration check: day 1 is worse at every time since the last calibration (+1.1 points in the first hour after one, +4.8 points at 12 to 24 h), and the raw sensor error just before each calibration is 19.3 % on day 1 vs 11.9 % on day 7. So calibration timing is not the cause; day-1 sensors drift away from each calibration faster.
- Mistake caught on the way: the first count of calibrations per session-day gave 2.4 on day 1, which cannot be right (two start-up entries plus two 12-hour ones). The start-up calibrations sit in the warm-up gap before the first reading, so a match on the first reading had assigned them to the previous session. Fixed by opening each session 30 min before its first reading; day 1 now has 4.4. No other result used that match.
- What changed in thinking: the midterm guess was "a sensor that fails is bad from the start". For the average error that is a minor part. The honest line for the paper is that day 1 is a settling period for every sensor, and that a day-1 cluster of complaints is expected even when no sensor is defective, because day 1 has 1.5 times the large errors and twice the false lows. The sensors users pull on day 1 are worse, but too few to move the trial average.
- Caveat to carry: fingersticks are not random (7 % more on day 1, 60 % more per hour on sensors that were pulled), and restarts of the same sensor cannot be told from replacements.
