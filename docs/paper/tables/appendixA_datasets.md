# Appendix A. More detail on the data sources and on responsible use

**REPLACE-BG.** The trial ran at 14 clinics in the United States [7], [8]. Its 226 participants were 114 men and 112 women, with a median age of 43 years and a median of 21.5 years with diabetes. After the run-in phase, 149 participants were asked to dose from the sensor alone and 77 to check with a meter first. Our main analysis uses all 226 participants (168,990 pairs), and the 77 who checked routinely (33,467 pairs) are the check. We left out the sensor readings from before enrolment (7.4 % of all sensor readings) and 50 meter values outside 20 to 599 mg/dL. We kept the readings at the two ends of the sensor's display range, 39 and 401 mg/dL (0.5 % of all sensor readings). Nobody with severe low glucose in the past 12 months could enter the trial, so the results may not hold for people at higher risk.

**ShanghaiT1DM/ShanghaiT2DM.** The patients were recruited between 2019 and 2021 and wore the sensor for 3 to 14 days [11]. The data hold a sensor reading every 15 minutes, meter values, ketone values, meal records and insulin doses. The type 1 group has 12 patients (7 women and 5 men, aged 37 to 73, mean age 58), and 10 of them have a subtype called latent autoimmune diabetes in adults. The type 2 group has 100 patients (44 women and 56 men, aged 22 to 97, mean age 60). Their median HbA1c was 8.5 % (HbA1c is a blood test for the average glucose of about three months, given in percent). Several laboratory tests are missing in 20 to 50 % of the type 2 records, so the patient traits are incomplete.

**FDA Records.** We found 32 recall events for glucose sensors, from six companies since 2008. A recall event is one FDA action and can cover several product records. Seven events are in Class I, the most serious class. In July 2024, 8,174 Libre 3 sensors could read too high [4]. In November 2025, 2,990,939 Libre 3 and Libre 3 Plus sensors, listed in four FDA records, could read too low [3]. The loss of signal in the Libre 3 app that the Introduction names is a separate Class II recall from February 2023 [93]. Of the 123,607 serious reports, 18,292 name the Libre 3 or Libre 3 Plus [14]. Reports can be incomplete, repeated or unverified, and makers differ in how they file them, so we read them as safety signals and not as failure rates [13]. We had no recorded readings from the recalled sensors, only the values that reports state, so we could not measure their accuracy.

**Independent Observations and Statistical Considerations.** In both clinical datasets the independent case is the person and not the pair. Pairs from one person share the same body, the same sensors with their calibration and the same testing habits, so we treated them as repeated observations and redrew whole participants for every 95 % interval. For the FDA records the case is one recall event or one report, and each report is counted once. An AI assistant wrote the labels of the 287 reference reports. One author read 100 of them one by one and changed no label, so a person has checked only part of the labels.

**Responsible Data Use.** We obtained the REPLACE-BG data from the public dataset page of the Jaeb Center for Health Research under its terms of use, and we do not redistribute them [7], [8]. We obtained the Shanghai data from Figshare under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 licence (CC BY 4.0), which allows reuse with credit to the authors [11], [12]. The FDA reports and recall records come from public FDA databases [3], [4], [14], [93]. The trial data and the Shanghai data are de-identified, and we made no attempt to identify anyone. From the report texts we took the device, the readings and the outcome, and we quote no personal details.

**Study Limitations.** Meter checks are not random moments, because people test when they feel low or doubt the sensor. A meter also has an error of its own. The standard ISO 15197:2013 asks that at least 95 % of a meter's results lie within 15 mg/dL of the reference method below 100 mg/dL, and within 15 % of it at 100 mg/dL or above [31]. A difference between sensor and meter is therefore a disagreement between two devices and not the sensor's error alone. Our simulations assume that the recorded sensor trace is the true glucose, so they show what an added fault changes and not the decisions people really made. The sources also differ in sensor, patients, calibration and years. Neither clinical dataset includes the recalled Libre 3 or Libre 3 Plus sensors, so our findings cannot be tied to those sensors or to their production lots.
